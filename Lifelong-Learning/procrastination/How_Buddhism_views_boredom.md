# How Buddhism views boredom

Buddhism views _boredom_ not as an enemy, but as _a form of craving (_tanha_) or aversion (_dosa_)_—a restless desire for constant stimulation and an unwillingness to be fully present with the "here and now."

## Common "Boring" Tasks & Practices in Buddhism

* **Mundane Daily Chores:** Washing dishes, sweeping floors, making the bed, and weeding. In Zen Buddhism, these repetitive chores are treated as primary meditative practices rather than interruptions to real life.
* **Chop Wood, Carry Water:** A famous Zen adage highlighting that enlightenment does not magically erase ordinary labor; you still have to do basic, repetitive chores before and after spiritual breakthroughs.
* **Repetitive Prostrations & Recitations:** Practices like performing hundreds of physical prostrations while tracking visualizations or lineages (such as in Tibetan _Lam-rim_ or guru pujas) can easily feel monotonous or tedious if the mind wanders. 
* **Seiza and Sitting Still:** Long periods of formal seated meditation (_seiza_ or zazen) can initially bring up physical numbness, agitation, and extreme mental boredom as the mind starves for external input.

## How Buddhism Transforms Boredom

* **Unmasking Craving:** According to community consensus on [Buddhism Stack Exchange](https://buddhism.stackexchange.com/questions/24822/what-does-buddhism-say-about-boredom), boredom is essentially a craving for energy, novelty, or distraction.
* **Observing Sensations:** Instead of reaching for a phone or fleeing the moment, mindfulness teaches you to look directly at boredom: feel its physical texture, restlessness, and tension in the body without judging it.
* **Present-Moment Depth:** When you stop resisting the task, the label of "boring" dissolves. As Jack Kornfield notes on [Jack Kornfield's Website](https://jackkornfield.com/boredom/), softly naming the feeling and staying embodied turns a dull moment into a rich space for awareness.
